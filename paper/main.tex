\title{Equilibrium or Retrieval? Do LLM Agents Derive or Recall Game-Theoretic Equilibria,\\
and What That Means for Mechanism Design}
\author{Meet Mehta}
\date{Draft, \today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) play textbook games well, but a model can reach the textbook
answer in two ways: by deriving it from the rules, or by recognising a famous game and
recalling its answer. We separate the two with \emph{isomorphic twins}: every question is
asked in its standard wording, in a disguised wording with identical numbers and rules but no
game vocabulary, and, in a third pre-registered study, in an extreme disguise with no story, coded
answers and a neutral answer box. Four models from four providers answer 18{,}768 questions
across a Vickrey auction, a Cournot duopoly, a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma (PD) and a
public goods game, each checked by a competence probe. All three studies are pre-registered on
OSF. We fit a logit quantal response equilibrium (QRE) to every cell and compare precision
$\lam$ across wordings. For the first model analysed (Nemotron~3~Ultra), the standard disguise
changed nothing: play was identical in both wordings, and the model named the disguised game in
54--100\% of answers, so the disguise did not hide the game. \tbd{2A results for the other
three models; Stage 2C (extreme disguise) results.} We then ask what noisy, QRE-like agents
imply for mechanism design. We prove that in a second-price auction a payment multiplier
$\alpha$ is irrelevant for QRE bidders, but not for bidders who recall ``bid your value'',
so $\alpha$ separates derivation from retrieval. We show that the revenue-optimal reserve for
QRE bidders is U-shaped in $\lam$, but gains at most about 1\% over Myerson's reserve. And we
derive in closed form the cooperation subsidy that reaches any target cooperation rate in a PD.
In the stage game, its halfway point is free of parameters for the whole regular QRE family. A pre-registered test
compares the derived subsidy's predictions with a classically ``guessed'' one: \tbd{Stage 5 result}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

LLM agents are beginning to take part in settings that game theory was built to analyse:
bidding, pricing, negotiating, and sharing common resources. Designing the rules for such
settings, which is the job of mechanism design, needs a model of how the participants behave.
Classical mechanism design assumes rational players who best-respond. Behavioural mechanism
design replaces them with an empirically fitted model, such as the logit quantal response
equilibrium \citep{mckelvey1995qre,goeree2016qre}. Before a fitted model of LLM play can be
trusted to design anything, a prior question must be answered: \emph{is the play we measure
reasoning at all, or retrieval?}

The concern is concrete. The Vickrey auction, the Cournot duopoly, the prisoner's dilemma and the
public goods game are among the most written-about games in any training corpus. A model can
answer ``bid your true value'' or ``produce $(a-c)/3b$'' because it has seen the answer
thousands of times, not because it derived it. LLMs are known to perform better on familiar
versions of a task than on unfamiliar but equivalent ones \citep{mccoy2024embers}. If strategic
play is recalled, then a mechanism tuned to it may fail as soon as the rules depart from the
textbook, which is exactly when a designer would change them.

\paragraph{What we do.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Isomorphic twins.} Each question has a standard (``named'') wording and a disguised
  wording. The disguise keeps every number and rule but removes the game's vocabulary. A third
  study adds an \emph{extreme} disguise: no story, coded answers (for example, offer $=3x+5$) and
  a neutral answer box. A model that derives should play identically across wordings; a model
  that recalls should not.
\item \textbf{Competence probes and pre-registration.} Every answer is paired with a short maths
  check posed in the question's own terms, and only answers whose check is passed are analysed.
  All hypotheses, sample sizes and analyses were pre-registered on OSF before data collection,
  in three registrations \citep{nosek2018prereg}.
\item \textbf{Behavioural fits.} We fit a logit QRE to each model, game and wording, and compare
  precision $\lam$ across wordings ($\Delta\lam$).
\item \textbf{Mechanism design for noisy agents.} We derive three results for QRE players: that
  a payment multiplier is irrelevant in a second-price auction; that a reserve price can only
  lower efficiency, while the revenue-optimal reserve is U-shaped in $\lam$; and a closed-form
  cooperation subsidy for the PD. We then test the derived subsidy on the models against a
  classically guessed one.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) A pre-registered, probe-checked twin design that separates derived from recalled strategic
play. (ii) Evidence on four frontier models across four games and three wordings
\tbd{one-sentence headline once all models are analysed}. (iii) Theory results for mechanism
design with QRE agents, including a parameter-free prediction (Corollary~1) and an
$\alpha$-based test of retrieval (Theorem~1), with a confirmatory test on the same models.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{LLMs as economic and strategic agents.} \citet{horton2023homo} proposed treating LLMs
as simulated economic agents, ``homo silicus'', and reproduced classic behavioural-economics
experiments with them. \citet{akata2025repeated} studied LLMs in finitely repeated $2\times 2$
games, including the PD, and found strong play in self-interested games but poor coordination.
\citet{fan2024rational} evaluated whether LLMs satisfy basic rationality requirements in
games, and benchmarks such as GTBench \citep{duan2024gtbench} measure strategic reasoning across
many game types. Our design differs: we hold the game fixed and vary only its \emph{wording},
so that a difference in play isolates recognition of the game rather than ability.

\paragraph{Behavioural game theory for LLMs.} \citet{jia2025behavioral} evaluated 22 LLMs with
behavioural game-theory models rather than Nash benchmarks. \citet{pechon2026auditing} audit
QRE-based measures of LLM strategic reasoning and argue that fitted parameters are payoff- and
model-dependent summaries rather than structural measures of rationality. We take that caution
seriously: we report fits per game rather than pooling one $\lam$; we verify by parameter
recovery that our fitting procedure recovers known $\lam$; and we use $\lam$ mainly for
\emph{within-question} comparisons across wordings, where the payoffs are identical by
construction.

\paragraph{Familiarity versus reasoning.} \citet{mccoy2024embers} showed that LLM accuracy
depends on how probable a task and its output are under the training distribution. Our twins
apply this idea to strategic play, with the important addition of competence probes, so that a
drop under disguise cannot simply reflect misreading the disguised rules.

\paragraph{Mechanism design.} Our auction results build on the second-price auction
\citep{vickrey1961}, on Myerson's optimal auction and implementability results
\citep{myerson1981}, and on the treatment in \citet{roughgarden2016}. QRE was introduced by
\citet{mckelvey1995qre} and is surveyed in \citet{goeree2016qre}. To our knowledge, the closed
forms and invariance results in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} are not stated in this form for
LLM agents elsewhere.

\section{Games, twins and competence probes}\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Games.} Four games with known, unique equilibrium actions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vickrey auction:} three bidders; private value uniform on $[10,100]$; the
  equilibrium is to bid one's value.
\item \textbf{Cournot duopoly:} inverse demand $P = a - bQ$ with $b = 1$ and $c = 10$, and $a$
  drawn per question; equilibrium quantity $(a-c)/3b$.
\item \textbf{Repeated PD:} 10 rounds, a horizon known to both players, payoffs
  $(T,R,P,S) = (5,3,1,0)$, asked in round~1; backward induction gives defection throughout.
\item \textbf{Public goods:} $n$ players, endowment $e$, multiplier $1 < M < n$; the equilibrium
  is to contribute nothing.
\end{itemize}
Numbers are drawn from seeded samplers, so each question can be reproduced exactly.

\paragraph{Twins.} The disguised wording keeps every number, rule and information structure (who
knows what, and who moves when) and removes the game's vocabulary (``auction'', ``firm'',
``cooperate''). The PD's labels are counterbalanced, with \texttt{X}/\texttt{Y} randomly assigned
to cooperation. Each wording has three paraphrases, which reword or reorder but never add or
drop a fact, so a wording effect can be told apart from a disguise effect. The \emph{extreme}
disguise (Stage 2C) goes further:
\begin{itemize}
\item no story at all;
\item coded answers (Vickrey offer $= 3x + 5$ with a private prize; Cournot amount $= 2x + 4$
  with the profit written in expanded form; public goods asks how much to \emph{keep});
\item PD payoffs transformed to $2u - 4$ and shown as balance changes;
\item a neutral answer box.
\end{itemize}
Each extreme question uses the same seed and paraphrase index as its standard twin, so every
question exists in three wordings with identical numbers.

\paragraph{Competence probes.} After each answer, the model receives a short calculation in the
same wording (for example, ``you bid 50, the highest other bid is 60; what is your payoff?'').
Only answers whose probe is passed are analysed. Verdicts are \textsc{at\_equilibrium},
\textsc{strategic\_gap} (probe passed, equilibrium missed), \textsc{competence\_gap} (probe
failed), and parse or format failures. Server refusals are infrastructure failures and are
re-asked, never scored.

\paragraph{Models.} gpt-oss-120b (via Groq), Nemotron~3~Ultra (NVIDIA), Kimi~K3 (Moonshot, via
NVIDIA) and Gemini~3.5~Flash~Lite (Google), all with default sampling, collected
September--November 2026.

\paragraph{Studies.} Three pre-registered studies, 18{,}768 questions in total:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Stage 2A} (pre-registration 1): 4 games $\times$ 2 wordings $\times$ 138 questions
  $\times$ 4 models $=$ 4{,}416.
\item \textbf{Stage 2B} (pre-registration 2): 12{,}144 questions in three blocks:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{traps}, variants whose textbook answer is wrong (for example, the winner pays
    \emph{twice} the second bid, or there are three firms instead of two);
  \item a \emph{number sweep} that varies one parameter;
  \item a PD \emph{endgame} asked at rounds 5, 9 and 10 after mutual cooperation.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Stage 2C} (pre-registration 3): the extreme disguise, 2{,}208 questions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Pre-registration and analysis}\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} Pre-registrations 1--3 were locked by SHA-256 fingerprint in git
and registered on OSF before the corresponding data existed. Each fixes:
\begin{itemize}
\item the predictions, written by the author;
\item the exact sample sizes, as a stopping rule;
\item exclusions;
\item the tests.
\end{itemize}
The analysis code was fingerprinted too, and it refuses to run on real data unless its
fingerprint matches. Ten deviations, all found and recorded before the affected analysis, are
listed in Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}.

\paragraph{Main tests.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Stage 2A:} equilibrium play, named vs disguised, two-proportion $z$-tests with
  Holm correction across games \citep{holm1979}. Public goods, the predicted null, uses an
  equivalence test (TOST, $\pm 13$ points).
\item \textbf{Stage 2C:} each extreme question is paired with its normal and disguised twins;
  exact McNemar tests, paired differences with Agresti--Min intervals
  \citep{agresti2005matched}, and pooling across models by summing discordant pairs (the
  Mantel--Haenszel test for matched pairs).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{QRE fitting.} In a logit QRE a player chooses action $a$ with probability
proportional to $\exp(\lam\, u(a))$, where $u$ is the expected payoff against the others'
equilibrium behaviour. $\lam = 0$ is uniform noise and $\lam \to \infty$ is Nash play. The
game-specific choices were fixed before fitting:
\begin{itemize}
\item the PD and Cournot use symmetric fixed points;
\item in public goods, $u(g) = (M/n - 1)g$;
\item in the Vickrey auction, rivals are assumed to bid their equilibrium bids, with values
  uniform on $[10,100]$.
\end{itemize}
We compute a posterior on a log-uniform grid $\lam \in [10^{-3}, 10^{3}]$. A cell whose evidence
keeps rising towards the top of the grid gets a lower bound only, and is flagged as a ceiling.
Parameter recovery on simulated data gave 93.75\% coverage of nominal 95\% intervals across
320 fits.

\paragraph{Reasoning codes.} The models' stated reasoning is coded by a fixed, locked codebook.
The key code is \textsc{names\_game}: the reasoning names the game, using a name that the question
itself does not use. Agreement with blind hand coding by the author was $\kappa = 1.00$
on Stage~2A wording. In the extreme wording, a blind second coding of 40 answers by an AI
coder (Claude), not a human, also agreed on all 40.

\paragraph{The fraction slip.} With a number-only answer box, Nemotron sometimes derives a
fraction ($683/60 \approx 11.38$) and writes only part of it (683) into the box. A mechanical
correction rule was fixed in pre-registration 3, before any Stage 2C data, and is applied
identically to both stages. It changed 25 of Nemotron's 276 Stage 2A Cournot answers, all to
correct, and no answer from any other model. Pre-registration 1's own analysis is reported
uncorrected, as registered.

\subsection{The disguise did not hide the game}

Nemotron \emph{named} the disguised game in 100\% (Vickrey), 96\% (Cournot), 81\% (PD) and 54\%
(public goods) of its disguised answers. A disguise that the model sees through cannot test
derivation against recall. Two causes were found:
\begin{itemize}
\item the disguised answer box still used the named game's field names (``bid'', ``quantity'',
  ``contribution''), recorded as deviation~8;
\item strong models recognise the mathematical structure itself.
\end{itemize}
This motivated the extreme disguise. In a pilot on practice questions, it cut Nemotron's naming
of Cournot from 96\% to 14\% and of the PD from 81\% to 50\%, while maths-check pass rates were
unchanged.

\paragraph{Why the equilibrium was missed.} The misses are informative:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cournot:} every named-wording miss, and 14 of 17 disguised misses, was a fraction
  slip, i.e.\ correct reasoning written into the box incorrectly.
\item \textbf{PD:} every named-wording cooperative choice gave a cooperation-norm reason (trust,
  mutual benefit), even though 91\% of PD answers mentioned backward induction.
\end{itemize}
So Nemotron's departures from equilibrium are not symmetric noise. They are formatting errors
(Cournot) and a systematic norm (PD).

\section{Mechanism design for noisy agents}\label{sec:mechanism}

We now treat each player as a logit QRE player with precision $\lam$ and ask how the rules
should change. Proofs of Theorems~1--3 were worked through by the author (see the contributions statement).

\subsection{Second-price auctions with a reserve and a payment multiplier}

Let $M(r,\alpha)$ be the rule under which the highest bid wins if it is at least $r$, and the
winner pays $\alpha \cdot \max(\text{second bid}, r)$. $M(0,1)$ is the Vickrey auction. Under
$M(0,\alpha)$ the dominant strategy is $b = v/\alpha$.

\begin{theorem}[$\alpha$ is irrelevant for QRE bidders]
If $(B_1,\dots,B_n)$ is a QRE of $M(r,1)$ on bid space $[0,\bar B]$, then
$(B_1/\alpha,\dots,B_n/\alpha)$ is a QRE of $M(r/\alpha,\alpha)$ on $[0,\bar B/\alpha]$, with
the same winner, revenue and payoffs.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Against a highest rival bid $H$,
$U_\alpha(b\mid v) = \mathbb{E}[(v - \alpha H)\mathbf 1\{H < b\}]$.
With $H = H_1/\alpha$ and $b = \beta/\alpha$,
$U_\alpha(\beta/\alpha \mid v) = U_1(\beta\mid v)$. So the $M_\alpha$ bid has density
$\propto \exp(\lam U_1(\alpha b\mid v))$, and by a change of variables, $\alpha B$ has the
$M_1$ QRE density. The scaled profile is therefore self-consistent. Dividing every bid by
$\alpha$ keeps their order, and revenue is $\alpha \cdot B_{(2)}/\alpha = B_{(2)}$.
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{Consequence: $\alpha$ is a test of retrieval.} For a deriving QRE bidder,
$\alpha \cdot b$ has the same distribution at every $\alpha$. A bidder that recalls ``bid your
value'' bids $v$ whatever $\alpha$ is. With three such bidders under $\alpha = 2$, the winner
expects $\mathbb E[v_{(1)}] - 2\,\mathbb E[v_{(2)}] = 77.5 - 110 = -32.5$. Stage 2B's
``pays twice'' trap is exactly this test: \tbd{trap result}.

\begin{theorem}[a reserve never raises efficiency]
For any $\lam$, expected efficiency (the winner's value, or 0 if there is no sale) cannot be
increased by $r > 0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For fixed bids, $r$ never changes who has the highest bid; it can only cancel the sale, which
replaces a value of at least 10 by 0. Under QRE the bids also respond to $r$. Solving the full
symmetric QRE numerically, efficiency falls in $r$ at every $\lam$ tested.
\end{proof}

So the originally planned problem, maximising efficiency over $(r,\alpha)$, has a trivial
answer. For \emph{revenue}, rational bidders with values uniform on $[10,100]$ have virtual
value $\varphi(v) = 2v - 100$ and Myerson's reserve $r^* = 50$. Table~\ref{tab:reserve} gives
$r^*(\lam)$ for QRE bidders, from the full symmetric QRE (each bidder responding to the others'
noisy bids).

$r^*(\lam)$ is U-shaped:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Moderate noise:} 21\% of bidders with $v > 50$ bid below a reserve of 50 (at
  $\lam = 0.2$), so lowering $r$ recovers those sales.
\item \textbf{Extreme noise:} bids barely track values (a bidder worth 20 bids 59 on average at
  $\lam = 0.02$), and the reserve becomes a floor under random overbids.
\end{itemize}
The gain over Myerson's reserve is at most about 1\% of revenue. And for Nemotron, whose auction
play is at the ceiling, there is nothing to correct. The auction is therefore not where a
behaviourally derived mechanism can matter for these models.

\subsection{A cooperation subsidy for the prisoner's dilemma}

Under rule $S(\tau)$, every player who cooperates is paid an extra $\tau$ each round. Against
an opponent who cooperates with probability $p$, defecting pays more by
$\Delta U(p) = p(T-R) + (1-p)(P-S)$. Under $S(\tau)$ this advantage becomes $\Delta U(p) - \tau$,
and the symmetric QRE satisfies $p = 1/(1+\exp(\lam(\Delta U(p)-\tau)))$.

\begin{theorem}[closed-form subsidy]
The subsidy at which the symmetric logit QRE cooperation rate equals $p^*$ is
\[
\tau(p^*,\lam) = \Delta U(p^*) + \frac{\logit(p^*)}{\lam}.
\]
Since $d\tau/dp = (T-R)-(P-S) + 1/(\lam p(1-p)) > 0$, each $\tau$ gives a unique QRE.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Rearrange: $(1-p)/p = \exp(\lam(\Delta U(p) - \tau))$, so $-\logit(p) = \lam(\Delta U(p)-\tau)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[a parameter-free halfway point]
At $\tau_{1/2} = \Delta U(\tfrac12) = \tfrac12[(T-R)+(P-S)]$ (that is, $1.5$ for our payoffs),
every regular QRE population cooperates with probability exactly $\tfrac12$, whatever its
precision and whatever the (symmetric) error distribution.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
An indifferent regular quantal responder randomises evenly. Uniqueness follows because
$\Delta U(p) - \tau$ rises in $p$ while the response falls in it.
\end{proof}

Rational players defect for $\tau < P - S = 1$ and cooperate for $\tau > T - R = 2$. In between,
the subsidised stage game is chicken, whose symmetric equilibrium mixes with $p = \tau - 1$, so
it too passes through $\tfrac12$ at $\tau = 1.5$. The corollary is a statement about the
\emph{stage} game. Chicken has two pure equilibria that pay each player differently, so in the
10-round game round-1 cooperation can be subgame-perfect: play $(C,C)$ and punish a deviator in
the last round \citep{benoit1985finitely}. At $\tau = 1.5$ deviating gains $0.5$, while the
last-round punishment costs at least $1.5$. Cooperation above 50\% at $\tau = 1.5$ is therefore
consistent with rational repeated-game play, as well as with a cooperation norm. Noisy players are cheap to nudge towards
partial cooperation and expensive to push to near-certainty. At Nemotron's fitted
$\lam = 0.154$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\tau = 1.5$ gives 50\%;
\item $\tau = 2.5$, the ``classical'' choice just above 2, gives only about 54\%;
\item 90\% needs $\tau \approx 16.2$.
\end{itemize}

\section{A confirmatory test of the derived mechanism}\label{sec:stage5}

The original plan's headline comparison is a mechanism derived from the fitted behaviour
against a naively guessed one. Stage~5 asks each model the Stage~2A PD (round 1, standard
wording) with the subsidy stated as a separate rule for both players, at four levels:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\tau = 0$ (baseline);
\item $\tau = 1.5$ (Corollary~1);
\item $\tau = 2.5$ (the classical guess);
\item $\tau^*(0.9, \hat\lam_m)$, Theorem~3's subsidy for 90\% cooperation at model $m$'s own
  fitted $\lam$ (the derived mechanism).
\end{itemize}
There are 100 questions per level per model, balanced across the three paraphrases, each with a
maths check that requires applying the subsidy. The design, predictions and tests will be
pre-registered (pre-registration 4) before any subsidy question is asked.

``More subsidy gives more cooperation'' would test nothing: $\tau^* = 1.9 + \ln 9/\lam > 2.5$
whenever $\lam < 3.66$. The derived-vs-guessed comparison is therefore between the two theories'
\emph{predictions}:
\begin{itemize}
\item at $\tau = 2.5$, is QRE's prediction closer to the observed rate than classical theory's
  100\%?
\item at $\tau^*$, is the observed rate within $90 \pm 15$\%?
\end{itemize}
The stage-game halfway point at $\tau = 1.5$ is read with the repeated-game caveat above. The
reasoning codes separate a cooperation norm (fairness) from repeated-game reasoning (future
rounds, reciprocity).

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Disguises can fail.} The standard disguise did not hide the games from the first
  model analysed. The extreme disguise hides some games (Cournot) better than others (Vickrey
  and public goods, whose rules are their fingerprint), so a null result under a disguise the
  model sees through says nothing about derivation.
\item \textbf{Timing.} Stage 2C was collected weeks after Stage 2A, so a provider updating a
  model in between would show up as a difference.
\item \textbf{The behaviour model.} QRE treats every departure from equilibrium as payoff-scaled
  noise. Nemotron's departures are not: they are formatting slips and a cooperation norm. Fitted
  $\lam$ should therefore be read as a summary, not as a structural parameter
  \citep{pechon2026auditing}.
\item \textbf{The stage-game QRE in the PD.} It ignores continuation play in the 10-round game.
  That is harmless for rational players when the stage equilibrium is unique ($\tau = 0$,
  $2.5$, $\tau^*$). It is not harmless at $\tau = 1.5$, where round-1 cooperation can be
  subgame-perfect.
\item \textbf{Four models.} With four models, cross-model patterns are rankings, not
  correlations.
\item \textbf{Free tiers.} All models were accessed through free tiers with default sampling.
  Provider-side changes, rate limits and outages were logged, and outages were re-asked rather
  than scored (Appendix~\ref{app:deviations}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Deviations from the pre-registrations}\label{app:deviations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{PD scoring error in practice runs:} named answers were scored against the
  disguised labels. It was fixed before Stage 2.
\item \textbf{Gemini~3.8~Flash replaced} by Gemini~3.5~Flash~Lite, because of a 20-request daily
  cap.
\item \textbf{Empty replies} (bursts of ``!!!!'') are re-asked, not scored as unreadable.
\item \textbf{gpt-oss's daily token cap} slows its collection; this does not affect the design.
\item \textbf{A 10-minute NVIDIA access outage:} its 4{,}454 refused requests were relabelled as
  infrastructure failures and re-asked.
\item \textbf{The fraction slip:} recorded as an exploratory observation, and corrected only in
  the pre-registration-3 comparison.
\item \textbf{Block~D (reasoning effort) dropped} before any of its data existed, so that all
  models answer the same questions.
\item \textbf{The disguised answer boxes} carried the named game's field names (a limitation).
\item \textbf{Ten questions re-asked} that should have been scored as the model's own (1 Kimi
  Stage~2A; 2 Nemotron Stage~2A; 7 Nemotron Stage~2B traps). The re-asked answers stand. The
  cases are \tbd{listed by question ID}.
\item \textbf{The pilot answer form} sent explanatory text that leaked words; it was fixed before
  Stage~2C.
\end{enumerate}
